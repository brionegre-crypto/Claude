For Black Christian men with ADHD

Your brain isn't the problem. The system you were handed is.

You love God. You mean what you say. And you've started the plan again, stalled again, and told yourself it's a character flaw. With ADHD, it usually isn't. Be The Man is a faith-first system built around the way an ADHD brain actually works.

Diagnosed or still wondering. You don't need a diagnosis to start.

Sound familiar?

You start strong. By day three something breaks, and the whole plan goes down with it.

“I'll do it Thursday” turns into Saturday, and you never felt the days go by.

Starting is the wall. Once you begin, you can go for hours.

You've downloaded the app, bought the planner, set the alarm. Each one worked for two weeks.

You've been told to try harder and pray harder. You did both.

Sunday night you add up what you didn't do, and it feels like a verdict.

None of that measures your faith or your worth. It's information about how your brain works, and information is something you can build around.

What's actually going on

ADHD is a difference in how the brain starts, plans, switches and keeps track of things. Researchers call these skills executive function. It's wiring, not character, and it has nothing to do with how much you love God or your family. It also comes with real strengths: many men with ADHD can go for hours on something that matters to them.

The trouble is that most productivity advice was written for a different brain.

The numbers.

6.0%

of US adults, about 15.5 million people, reported a current ADHD diagnosis in a 2023 CDC survey.

What the research says about plans

In two 2011 studies, children with ADHD who made if-then plans (“If X happens, then I will do Y”) did better on tasks that need attention-shifting, working memory and waiting for a bigger reward. Those studies were with children, and Be The Man itself has not been studied, so we say built on, not proven.

Sources: CDC, Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, October 10, 2024 (self-reported diagnoses, online survey, October to November 2023). Gawrilow, Gollwitzer & Oettingen (2011), Cognitive Therapy and Research 35:442-455, and Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology 30:616-646.

Why the usual advice fails you.

It runs on willpower

Most advice assumes you can hold a plan in your head and feel the days coming. For many men with ADHD that is exactly the hard part, and trying harder at it only adds shame.

It assumes every day is a good day

One plan, full speed, every day. When your energy swings, the plan snaps, and so does your confidence.

It turns a miss into a verdict

One missed day becomes proof you're lazy or faithless. A missed day is a data point, and a data point is something you can fix.

How Be The Man is built differently.

Six moves, each one small enough to start tonight.

1 · Get it out of your head

Put it where you can see it.

Step 1 is one written sentence about the man you're becoming. The Weekly Scorecard is one page you can look at. “Write the vision; make it plain on tablets, so he may run who reads it.” Habakkuk 2:2

2 · Time, place, trigger

Give it an address.

“This week” is not a time. “Thursday, 7:40 PM, phone in the drawer” is. Time is easier to feel when it has an address.

3 · The bad-day version

The smallest version that still counts.

Every commitment gets a bad-day version, so a rough week bends the plan instead of ending it.

4 · Define the miss

Count it. Don't judge it.

Decide ahead of time what a miss is. Four out of seven is four out of seven. Missing once is not failure. Not looking is.

5 · A brother alongside you

Starting is easier with company.

A partner, a one-word text each week (done, or missed), a monthly call, a room of men. For many men with ADHD, beginning is easier when someone's there.

6 · God at the center

Scripture is the standard.

What this is, and what it isn't

Be The Man is faith-based teaching and accountability. Brian Greene is a pastor of ten years and a certified marriage counselor. He is not a physician or a psychologist, and this is not medical or mental health treatment. It does not treat ADHD, and it does not replace an assessment, medication or therapy from a licensed clinician. You don't need a diagnosis to start.

If you think you may have ADHD, a licensed clinician can help you find out. If you are in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US).
