# Drift Is Quiet: How Good Marriages Slowly Turn Into Logistics

*Subtitle: Most couples don't fall apart in a blowup. They fade out in a hundred small, polite exchanges about who's picking up the kids.*

Nobody plans to drift.

There's no day on the calendar when a husband and wife decide to become roommates who happen to share a last name. It doesn't arrive with a fight, an affair, or a dramatic announcement. It arrives with Tuesday.

Tuesday becomes Wednesday. Work gets busy. The kids need things. You're tired, she's tired, and the easiest way to get through the day is to handle the list. Who's driving? Is the bill paid? Did you call the plumber?

Months pass. Then one night you sit across the table from the person you love and realize that you could recite her schedule from memory, but you couldn't tell her what she's been worried about lately.

That's drift. And the reason it's so dangerous is that it feels like nothing is wrong.

## Drift vs. fighting

Fighting is loud. It hurts, but it also tells you something is at stake. Two people who argue about the same issue are, in a strange way, still invested in each other.

Drift is the opposite. It's polite. There's no conflict because there's no contact. Nothing gets said because nothing real is being asked.

Here are five signs you may be drifting rather than fighting:

**1. Only logistics.** Almost every conversation is about schedules, kids, money, and chores.

**2. Phones at dinner.** Or in bed, or in the car, or on the couch while the TV plays.

**3. No real questions.** When did either of you last ask something you didn't already know the answer to?

**4. Rare touch.** Not just intimacy, but small physical affection: a hand on the back, a hug that lasts a second longer than it has to.

**5. Fights that feel pointless.** When you do argue, it's about the dishes, but somehow it feels bigger and emptier than the dishes.

If you recognized two or three of these, don't panic. You're not failing. You're a normal couple in a busy season. But you've probably been coasting, and coasting always goes downhill.

## Why men miss it

I'll speak to the husbands, because we're often the last to notice.

A lot of us measure marriage by whether the problems are being solved. The bills are paid, the house is standing, the kids are fed. If things are running, we assume things are fine.

But a wife rarely measures the marriage by the house. She measures it by whether she feels known. Whether you notice. Whether you'd choose her attention over your phone, her story over the game, her presence over your own comfort.

You can solve every problem on the list and still leave her feeling alone. That's the quiet trap.

## Small things that turn it around

The good news is that drift doesn't need a dramatic rescue. Because it was built out of small things, it can be reversed by small things.

**A five-minute check-in, nightly.** Phones in another room. Three questions: *What went well today? What was hard? What do you need from me tomorrow?* Then listen without fixing.

**One real question a day.** Not "how was your day," but "what's been on your mind this week?" or "what was the best part of yesterday?" Ask, then be quiet long enough to hear the answer.

**Phone in the kitchen at 9 PM.** It costs nothing. It gives the last hour of the day back to the people in your house.

**Say yes before she asks.** If she has asked for help three times, that's not nagging. It's a request that hasn't been honored yet. Honor it today, unprompted.

**Specific gratitude.** Not "thanks for everything," but "thank you for taking the kids so I could finish the garage." Specific praise lands because it proves you noticed.

**A scheduled date, with half of it not about the kids.** It doesn't need to be fancy. It needs to be on the calendar, because things off the calendar lose to things on it.

## When to bring in help

Sometimes drift has gone on long enough that good intentions aren't enough. Years of unspoken hurt don't dissolve in a five-minute check-in. If you've tried and the same walls keep going up, or if you're engaged and want to build a strong foundation before the wedding, that is not a sign of weakness. It's wisdom.

It also helps to see where you actually stand. Most couples guess about where the marriage is strong and where it's stretched. A validated assessment, walked through with a certified counselor, replaces guessing with a clear picture and a plan.

## Don't wait for a crisis

The strongest marriages I know aren't the ones that never struggled. They're the ones that noticed drift early and chose, repeatedly, to turn toward each other.

You're not against each other. You're for the same marriage. Say that out loud the next time things get tense, and then ask your spouse one real question you don't already know the answer to.

Tonight. Phones away. Five minutes.
