Read this first. Researchers who study pathological lying say plainly that no proven treatment exists yet, and they point to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) as the best place to start. So this guide is built from CBT methods with strong evidence for similar problems: tracking the behavior, if-then plans, habit reversal, exposure, relapse prevention, and research on shame, self-compassion and rebuilding trust. Every day links to the study behind it, rated honestly as Strong, Moderate or Early. It is a daily structure, not therapy. Do it alongside a licensed counselor; booking one is on Day 3. If you ever feel hopeless or think about harming yourself, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US).

Tonight: Day 0

Don't wait for Monday. Do these five things before you sleep.

  1. Write one sentence: "I am a man whose word holds, even when the truth costs me."
  2. Count honestly: roughly how many lies did you tell this week? Write the number.
  3. Find 2 or 3 licensed counselors and write their names. You'll book one by Day 3.
  4. Choose your accountability brother and text him: "I'm working on something. Can I send you my numbers once a week?"
  5. If she's open to it, tell your wife: "I've lied to you, and it comes from fear. I'm not asking you to trust me. I've started daily work and I'm booking a counselor this week. You'll see it, not just hear it."

Then do not tell another lie tonight.

The three lines

  1. No new lies. When the alarm goes off in your body, pause. Three seconds and one breath, then a true sentence.
  2. Correct it fast. If one gets out, fix it within 60 seconds, or the same day at the latest: “Actually, that's not true.”
  3. Log every one, every night. Being honest with the ledger is the first honesty. A ledger with a lie in it is worthless.

The pause: true sentences to use

A lie fills a silence. Have true sentences ready so you have something else to say.

When the urge hits“Give me a second. I want to answer that honestly.”
When you don't know“I don't know.” / “I'm not sure. Let me check and tell you.”
When you didn't do it“I didn't do it yet. I'll have it done by ___.”
When you failed“I messed that up. Here's what I'm doing about it.”
When it already slipped out“Actually, that's not true. The truth is ___.”
When you're ashamed“I'm embarrassed to tell you this, but…”
When she asks and you need time“I'll answer that fully. Give me until tonight.” Then answer it tonight.

The evening ledger

Use the Notes app or a paper notebook, one page per night. Numbers matter. Over 49 days you should see lies told go down while lies caught and corrections go up.

THE LEDGER (10 min)
1. Lies told today (#): what, to whom
2. Lies caught before I spoke (#)
3. Corrections made (#)
4. The trigger: who, where, how tired I was, and the fear (failure / rejection / conflict / looking weak)
5. The story in my head: "If I tell the truth, ___ will happen."
6. What actually happened when I told the truth
7. Tomorrow's risk moment, written as an if-then: "If ___, then I'll say ___."
8. One line of prayer or thanks

With your wife

  • Don't dump everything in a panic tonight. A rushed, emotional confession usually comes out in pieces and causes more damage. Plan the full truth with your counselor (Week 5).
  • Don't add any new lies. If she asks you something directly, don't lie. If you need time: “I'll answer that fully. I want to do it right, with the counselor.” Then follow through.
  • No trickle truth. Telling the truth a little at a time, each time you're caught, breaks trust again every time. One full disclosure is far better.
  • Don't ask for trust. Earn it. Never ask “When will you trust me?” Give her things she can check: kept promises, open access, your weekly numbers if she wants them.
  • Her pain isn't an attack. Stay in the room, listen, and don't argue about the details.
  • Consider couples counseling once your own work has started. Your counselor can tell you when.

The science behind the system

What researchers know about lying, habits, shame and trust, and exactly where each finding shows up in your 49 days.

Strong reviews of many experiments   Moderate solid studies, or a proven method borrowed from a related problem   Early small, mixed or unpublished evidence

Moderate evidence

Compulsive lying is real, and there is no proven cure yet

In a 2020 study of over 600 people, about 13% said they or others saw them as pathological liars: telling many lies every day for six months or more. They described the lying as compulsive and out of their control, said it reduced their anxiety in the moment, and said lies often grew out of one first lie. The authors note there is no evidence-based treatment yet and suggest cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) as the place to start.

In your system: This guide is built from CBT tools that work for similar problems. It is a structure for doing the work, and you should do it with a counselor.

Curtis & Hart (2020), Psychiatric Research and Clinical Practice · Used on Days 3, 13

Moderate evidence

Most lies come from a small group of people

In a survey of 1,000 U.S. adults, 60% reported telling no lies in the past 24 hours. A small group of prolific liars told a large share of all the lies.

In your system: Lying a lot is a pattern, not human nature. Patterns can be counted and changed. That's why Week 1 is counting.

Serota, Levine & Boster (2010), Human Communication Research · Used on Days 2

Moderate evidence

Trying to look competent or likable makes people lie more

When people in a recorded conversation were trying to come across as likable or competent, they told more lies than people with no such goal.

In your system: Your two fears, failing and not being accepted, are exactly these two goals. Week 2 teaches you to notice which one is running in the moment.

Feldman, Forrest & Happ (2002), Basic and Applied Social Psychology · Used on Days 4, 8, 9

Moderate evidence

Small lies make bigger lies easier

In a brain-imaging study, the brain's emotional alarm (the amygdala) reacted strongly to the first self-serving lie and less to each lie after it, while the lies grew bigger. The bigger the drop in that reaction, the bigger the next lie.

In your system: There's no such thing as a harmless small lie for you. Every exaggeration trains the slope. That's why Day 20 is "No rounding up."

Garrett, Lazzaro, Ariely & Sharot (2016), Nature Neuroscience · Used on Days 20

Strong evidence

Tracking your behavior changes your behavior

A review of 138 experiments with almost 20,000 people found that monitoring your progress helps you reach your goals. The effect was larger when progress was written down and when it was reported to someone else.

In your system: The nightly ledger is the written record. The weekly text with your numbers to your accountability brother is the reporting.

Harkin et al. (2016), Psychological Bulletin · Used on Days 1, 7, 21, 28, 48

Strong evidence

If-then plans work

A review of 94 experiments found that plans written as "If X happens, then I will do Y" substantially improved follow-through compared with good intentions alone.

In your system: Every morning you write one if-then plan for the day's risk moment: "If she asks about the money, then I'll say the real number."

Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), Advances in Experimental Social Psychology · Used on Days 18, 40

Moderate evidence

Break an automatic habit: notice it, then replace it

Habit reversal training (learning the early warning signs, practicing a replacement response, and getting support from someone) is a well-established treatment for automatic behaviors like tics and hair-pulling. It hasn't been tested on lying specifically.

In your system: Day 5 teaches you your body's alarm. The pause and a prepared true sentence are the replacement response. Your brother is the support.

Bate, Malouff, Thorsteinsson & Bhullar (2011), Clinical Psychology Review (meta-analysis) · Used on Days 5, 19

Early evidence

The pause interrupts an automatic response

Research on whether people are more honest when rushed or when they slow down is mixed. Results depend on the situation. But for compulsive liars, who describe their lies as automatic and out of their control, the goal is to interrupt the habit before the words come out.

In your system: The three-second pause isn't magic. It's the gap where your prepared true sentence gets a chance.

Kobis et al. (2019), Perspectives on Psychological Science; Curtis & Hart (2020) · Used on Days 15

Moderate evidence

Lies are safety behaviors. Dropping them teaches you that the truth is survivable

In CBT for social anxiety, "safety behaviors" protect people from what they fear but keep the fear alive, because they never get to see that the feared outcome doesn't happen. Dropping them during planned practice is a standard part of treatment.

In your system: Each lie you don't tell is a test of your prediction. The truth ladder (Week 4) and the prediction test (Day 12) are behavioral experiments.

Clark & Wells cognitive model; Morgan & Raffle (1999); Salkovskis · Used on Days 12, 14, 16, 22, 23, 26

Moderate evidence

Shame hides. Guilt repairs

Decades of research by June Tangney and others show that shame ("I am bad") tends to make people hide and withdraw, while guilt ("I did something bad") tends to make people take steps to repair. The pattern isn't perfect, but it's consistent.

In your system: Day 11 teaches you to turn shame sentences into guilt sentences. You can repair something you did. You can't repair who you are.

Tangney & Dearing, Shame and Guilt (2002) and later reviews · Used on Days 11, 24, 34

Moderate evidence

Self-compassion makes men more likely to make amends

After recalling something they had done wrong, people who wrote to themselves with kindness and understanding were more motivated to make amends and not repeat it than people who boosted their self-esteem or did nothing.

In your system: Beating yourself up feeds shame, and shame feeds hiding. After a slip, you write yourself two honest, kind sentences, then you go fix it.

Breines & Chen (2012), Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin · Used on Days 47, 49

Moderate evidence

A slip doesn't have to become a relapse

Relapse prevention research describes the "abstinence violation effect": after one slip, thinking "I'm a failure, it's no use" makes a full return to the old pattern more likely. Seeing a slip as a mistake you can correct protects against that.

In your system: If you lie, you correct it, log it, tell your brother, and keep going. You never go back to Day 1.

Marlatt & Gordon, Relapse Prevention; Larimer, Palmer & Marlatt (1999) · Used on Days 17, 45

Moderate evidence

Remembering your values makes you less defensive

When people first reflect on a value that matters to them, they're better able to hear threatening information without getting defensive, and more likely to change. The effect varies across studies.

In your system: Before a hard conversation with your wife, spend two minutes writing about why your faith or your children matter to you. Then go listen.

Sherman & Cohen (2002), Current Directions in Psychological Science · Used on Days 27, 38, 43

Moderate evidence

Secrets weigh on you even when nobody is asking

Across ten studies, the harm from a secret came mostly from how often people's minds drifted back to it, not from how often they had to actively hide it.

In your system: The lies you're still carrying cost you every day, not just when you're questioned. That's part of why Week 5 plans a full disclosure.

Slepian, Chun & Mason (2017), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology · Used on Days 25, 29

Moderate evidence

Writing about hard things helps a little

A review of 146 experiments found that writing about difficult experiences has a small but real benefit for health and well-being.

In your system: The evening questions and letters are writing exercises. They help, but they're one tool among several, not a cure.

Frattaroli (2006), Psychological Bulletin · Used on Days 10, 41

Early evidence

Tired men have less self-control

Studies at work found that people who slept less behaved less ethically, apparently because fatigue drains self-control. A later replication found no effect, so the evidence is mixed.

In your system: Treat being tired as a risk factor. Your evening ledger and your relapse warning signs include sleep.

Barnes, Schaubroeck, Huth & Ghumman (2011), Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes · Used on Days 44

Early evidence

Lying less goes with better health and relationships

In a 10-week experiment, weeks when people told fewer lies were weeks they reported better physical and mental health, mostly because their relationships improved. It was presented at a conference and wasn't published in a journal, so treat it as preliminary.

In your system: The payoff of this work isn't only your marriage. You should also start to feel better.

Kelly & Wang (2012), American Psychological Association convention · Used on Days 6, 46

Moderate evidence

Trust after lying comes back slowly, through actions

In a lab experiment, trust broken by bad actions alone came back after a consistent run of trustworthy actions. Trust broken by bad actions plus lies never fully recovered, even with a promise, an apology and consistent good behavior. A promise to change did speed things up.

In your system: Set your expectations: this is a long road, and words won't carry you. Make small, specific promises and keep them exactly (Day 40). Never demand trust (Day 39).

Schweitzer, Hershey & Bradlow (2006), Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes · Used on Days 32, 36, 37, 39

Early evidence

Full disclosure, with support, is usually seen as right afterward

In a survey of 164 people recovering from betrayal in their marriages, about 96% of those who disclosed and 93% of their partners said afterward that disclosure was the right decision. Most couples didn't separate. Partners said they needed more support from professionals during the process. The study was about infidelity, not lying in general.

In your system: Plan one full disclosure with your counselor (Week 5). Don't let it come out in pieces, and don't do it alone in a panic.

Schneider, Corley & Irons (1998), Sexual Addiction & Compulsivity · Used on Days 30, 33, 35

Moderate evidence

Couples therapy has tested models for repairing betrayal

Emotionally Focused Therapy has a tested process for resolving "attachment injuries" (betrayals at a moment of need), and an integrative couples model for affairs moves through three stages: dealing with the impact, understanding why it happened, and moving forward.

In your system: Once your own work is underway, ask your counselor about couples therapy with someone trained in EFT, the Gottman Method or affair-recovery work.

Makinen & Johnson (2006), Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology; Baucom, Snyder & Gordon · Used on Days 31, 42

Choosing a counselor

Because there's no proven treatment for compulsive lying yet, the counselor you choose matters. Look for:

  • A licensed clinician: a psychologist, LPC or LMFT. If your faith matters to you, look for one who is licensed and also comfortable working with Christian clients.
  • CBT-based methods, including exposure and behavioral experiments. Ask: “How would you treat lying that feels automatic and is driven by fear of failure and rejection?”
  • A real assessment. Ask them to check whether anxiety, depression, ADHD, trauma or something else is feeding the lying. Treating that can make everything else easier.
  • Later, a couples therapist trained in Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), the Gottman Method or affair-recovery work, when your counselor says it's time.
  • Bring this guide. Show them your ledger numbers and ask them to adjust the plan to fit you.
